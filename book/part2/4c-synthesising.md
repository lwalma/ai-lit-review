# Synthesising Findings

Next step in the analysis is synthesising, where you compare the different information you have found and connect your findings to your research question.  The key question in a synthesis is often: how do the resources you found and read relate to one another, in your interpretation and what variety of answers have you found for your information search questions?

## Recap Standard Practice: Organise Your Findings and Compare Sources

Based on your observations and your information search questions, group your sources in a way that makes sense for the question you want to answer or the argument you want to make. When grouping your sources, you integrate the different sources to tell your perspective on the literature you found. 

Rather than a summary, this is where you combine the sources to create, for example, a new overview of the literature on a topic (e.g. introduction or literature review), or combine the main findings in the sources with your own results to discuss your findings (e.g. discussion). 

What lens to focus on is related to your information search question. As you go comparing your sources and adding your own voice, decide what the important themes and answers you need from the literature are. Some examples of how you can group your sources:
- Discuss Methods used in Your Field
- Analyse important topics
- Showcase discussions in the field
- Present a chronological progression of a topic

After deciding what way you want to group and compare your sources, and what specific topics you are going to look for in the literature, you can start comparing the information in your sources. A helpful tool can be a Synthesis Matrix. In a synthesis matrix you list the sources you have selected for your project and compare their findings on topics you have selected. It could look like this, for example:

| Sub-topic or theme | Source 1 | Source 2 | Source 3 | My Conclusions |
|-|-|-|-|-|
| Sub-topic or theme 1 | relevant information from source 1 about theme 1 | relevant information from source 2 about theme 1 | relevant information from source 3 about theme 1 |  ... |
| Sub-topic or theme 2 | relevant information from source 1 about theme 2 | ... | ... | ... |
| Sub-topic or theme 3 | relevant information from source 1 about theme 3 | ... | ... | ... |

## Warning: The Use of AI for Synthesising Literature

While the use of AI is very helpful for exploring your field, finding more sources and supporting selection and reading, it is risky to use AI to do the complete analysis of the selected papers for you. There are multiple reasons why it is better to analyse papers yourself:

````{tab-set}

```{tab-item} Cognitive offloading

Having AI do your research limits your own learning process. Synthesising information is one of the key points where learning takes place. Synthesising the information yourself also helps you to get a better understanding of the topics you're working on.

```

```{tab-item} Risk of bias / inaccuracies

AI may introduce incorrect information or biased information. This is a common problem with AI tools. If you don't read and synthesise the literature yourself, there is a big risk that your analysis contains inaccuracies or simply doesn't make sense. Be reminded that AI tools don't think: they produce content based on prompts they receive and databases they are trained with. If you feed your sources to an AI, it doesn't understand the articles, and therefore will probably not be able to sysnthesise literature in a correct and meaningful way.

```
```{tab-item} Copyright issues

When you are doing your analyses you may be working with copyrighted or sensitive information. It is unclear where the information will end up, as such it is not recommended to upload copyrighted information to AI tools.

```
````
